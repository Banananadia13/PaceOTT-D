1
The Role of an OT at PACE & Reality of Community Practice
🏥
Part 1: What OTs at PACE Do

OTs support participants to improve:

  • Independence in daily activities
  • Safety within their environment
  • Participation in meaningful activities
  • Overall quality of life

Achieved through:

  • Functional assessments
  • Environmental & home modifications
  • Assistive technology
  • Capacity building interventions
🌐
Part 2: Reality of Community Practice

Participants rarely present with a single isolated issue. They often have:

  • Multiple diagnoses or conditions
  • Environmental and social barriers
  • Mental health challenges
  • Limited or inconsistent supports
  • Complex family or living situations
💡
Progress is often non-linear — dependent on multiple interacting variables. Clinicians must be comfortable with complexity.
2
Progress Over Perfection
🐢
Small, Incremental
Progress occurs through consistent, small improvements — not sudden leaps.
🔄
Layer Over Time
Interventions are introduced, tested, and built upon — not delivered all at once.
🎯
Identify Priority Areas
Break goals into manageable steps and reinforce small wins to build consistency.

Why This Model Matters

Pain and disability are not solely the result of tissue damage. They are shaped by context including beliefs, previous experiences, stress, and social support.

A BPS approach allows clinicians to:

  • Understand the full context of a participant's presentation
  • Identify multiple contributing factors
  • Move beyond single-cause explanations
  • Tailor interventions more effectively
4
Complicated vs Complex Presentations
🔧 Complicated
  • Difficult but follows predictable patterns
  • Can be addressed with clear rules or processes
  • Tends to have consistent outcomes when managed correctly
  • Example: mechanical or procedural tasks
⚙️
Think: A task with many steps — but each step is known.
🕸️ Complex — Most PACE Participants
  • Multiple interacting variables
  • No single clear solution
  • Produces unpredictable outcomes
  • Requires ongoing adaptation
🧩
Shift from: "solving" problems → learning to manage, trial, and adapt over time.
5
Ethical Model of Care
🛡️
Non-maleficence
Avoid unnecessary harm. Do not reinforce beliefs that increase dependency.
❤️
Beneficence
Act in the participant's best interest. Support meaningful, long-term outcomes.
🗳️
Autonomy
Support participants to make their own decisions. Encourage independence, not reliance.
📋
Informed Consent
Ensure participants understand their options, timelines, and realistic expectations.

2
The Decision Line
← NOT READY CLINICIAN ASSESSES READINESS READY →

❌ Not Ready for Action

  • Build understanding and insight
  • Explore concerns and fears
  • Support reflection and awareness
  • Don't push action — it creates resistance

✅ Ready for Action

  • Move to planning and goal setting
  • Implement strategies collaboratively
  • Build on momentum with small wins
  • Reinforce and review progress
3
Core MI Skills — OARS
O
Open Questions

Encourage exploration and discussion. Avoid yes/no answers.

"What would you like to be able to do more independently?"
A
Affirmations

Recognise the participant's strengths and efforts genuinely.

"You've already made some important progress — that takes real effort."
R
Reflective Listening

Demonstrate understanding and build therapeutic rapport.

"It sounds like you want to be more independent, but you're worried about making things worse."
S
Summaries

Clarify and reinforce key points from the conversation.

"So improving your confidence at home is really important to you right now."
4
Laws of Behaviour Change
👁️
Obvious
Clearly understood and visible. Participants know what to do and when.
🪶
Easy
Achievable within the participant's current capacity. Low barrier to starting.
😊
Enjoyable
Aligned with participant interests and preferences. They want to do it.
🏆
Satisfying
Provides a sense of achievement or reward. Creates positive reinforcement.
5
Understanding & Addressing Misconceptions
Type 1
🕳️ Missing Gap
Lack of understanding or information. The participant hasn't been given the full picture to make sense of their situation.
Type 2
🌾 Single Grain
Focusing on one piece of information without wider context. One experience or statement becomes the entire belief.
Type 3
🏰 Sandcastle
Unstable beliefs built on incorrect assumptions. Can shift with the right approach, but may crumble under direct challenge.
Type 4
🪨 Sandstone
Deeply ingrained and resistant beliefs. Requires sustained, empathetic engagement and gradual linking to meaningful goals.
⚠️
Key principle: Challenging beliefs directly may lead to resistance. Guide participants toward new understanding through discussion, reflection, and linking to their own goals.

Why Sustainability Matters in Clinical Practice
⚠️
When Clinicians Are Overwhelmed

Clinicians who are fatigued or overwhelmed are more likely to:

  • Miss key information in sessions
  • Over- or under-prescribe interventions
  • Avoid difficult conversations
  • Experience reduced confidence in decisions

Sustainability Is a Clinical Skill

Sustainability is not just about wellbeing — it is about maintaining consistent, high-quality clinical performance over time.

💡
Maintaining clinician sustainability directly supports better participant outcomes — not just better clinician wellbeing.

How These Domains Interact

Just as we understand participants through a biopsychosocial lens, clinician wellbeing can be considered across the same three domains.

  • Biological: Sleep quality, fatigue levels, physical health and recovery
  • Psychological: Stress, emotional load from complex cases, decision fatigue
  • Social: Caseload demands, support from peers and supervisors, workplace culture
💡
Understanding these interacting factors allows clinicians to identify where changes are needed and address them proactively.

5
Managing Workload, Emotional Load & Boundaries
📋
Workload Management
  • Focus on high-impact tasks first
  • Break complex tasks into manageable steps
  • Schedule dedicated documentation time
  • Allow buffer time between sessions
  • Balance efficiency with quality of care
💛
Managing Emotional Load
  • Recognise emotional responses as they arise
  • Reflect on experiences — use supervision
  • Seek support before load becomes unmanageable
  • Maintain professional boundaries consistently
Ignoring emotional load over time is a primary driver of burnout.
🔒
Setting Boundaries
  • Define realistic session goals upfront
  • Avoid over-commitment with participants
  • Maintain appropriate communication limits
  • Recognise when to refer or seek support
Boundaries protect both clinician wellbeing AND the quality of care delivered.

1
The PEO Model
P
Person
  • Physical abilities
  • Cognitive capacity
  • Emotional factors
  • Health conditions
E
Environment
  • Physical environment
  • Social supports
  • Accessibility
  • Cultural context
O
Occupation
  • Meaningful activities
  • Daily tasks
  • Roles and routines
🎯
Clinical reasoning involves identifying where the mismatch is occurring — between the person, their environment, and the occupation. This guides intervention selection.
2
Identifying Key Drivers — The Driver Hierarchy
🎯 PRIMARY DRIVER — Main factor limiting performance
🔶 SECONDARY DRIVERS — Contributing factors
⬜ BACKGROUND FACTORS — Relevant but not immediately actionable
🍳
Example: Difficulty Preparing Meals

A participant struggling with meal preparation may have multiple contributing factors:

  • Reduced grip strength
  • Fatigue limiting standing tolerance
  • Poor kitchen setup and equipment
  • Low confidence and fear of failure
❓
Key question: "Which of these is the main factor limiting performance right now?" Identify the primary driver first.
3
The Clinical Reasoning Loop
Assess
→
Interpret
→
Act
→
Review
↩
ASSESS
Gather information. Use PEO to explore all domains.
INTERPRET
Identify primary driver. Link factors to function.
ACT
Intervene, trial, or refer. Target the primary driver.
REVIEW
Evaluate outcomes. Adjust approach as needed.
4
Decision Tree — Act, Trial, or Refer?
Problem Identified via Clinical Reasoning
Strong evidence of benefit + clear clinical need + safety concern
✅ Act — Intervene Directly
Participant unsure + multiple options + benefit unclear + high cost
🔬 Trial — Test the Approach First
Outside OT scope + specialist expertise needed + safety risk without input
↗ Refer — Involve Another Discipline
💡
Also consider: Is the participant ready to engage with this intervention? What stage of change are they in? This links directly to Workshop 2.

Case-Based Discussion
Case 1
Applying the PEO Model — Reduced Capacity & Environmental Barriers

A participant presents with reduced physical capacity, significant environmental barriers at home, and low motivation. They have previously disengaged from services. They want to improve independence but are not sure where to start.

  1. How would you apply the PEO model to analyse this presentation?

You have identified the primary driver of a participant's difficulties. However, when you present your clinical reasoning, the participant appears disengaged and says "I've tried things before and they never work."

Discussion Questions

  1. What type of misconception might this participant have?

Key Takeaways

Clinical reasoning is the foundation of targeted, effective intervention
The reasoning loop — Assess, Interpret, Act, Review — guides structured thinking
Use the PEO model to identify where the mismatch is occurring
Always link reasoning to the participant's readiness for change
Identify the primary driver first — don't address everything at once
Clear decisions (act/trial/refer) reduce unnecessary interventions

Service Area Comparison
🦽 Assistive Technology

Use when:

  • Equipment supports participation
  • Independence can be gained with aids
  • Trial before formal prescription

Low vs High Cost:

  • Low: simpler, quicker
  • High: detailed assessment, funding required

Use when:

  • Environmental barriers are the primary driver
  • Safety risk is present
  • Long-term access improvement needed

  • Complex functional picture

  • Link findings to function

Use when:

  • Participant has potential to build skills
  • Confidence or routine is the main barrier

SOAP Note Documentation
S
SUBJECTIVE

What the participant reports

"Reports increased fatigue when cooking — needs to rest after 5 minutes."
