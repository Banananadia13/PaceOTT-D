01
Workshop One
The PACE Way –
Foundations of Clinical Practice
This workshop introduces the PACE approach to occupational therapy practice and sets expectations for how therapy progresses with participants in real-world clinical settings.

Learning Objectives

Understand the role of OT within PACE Health
Apply the "progress not perfection" mindset
Recognise the purpose of early therapy sessions
Understand how interventions are layered over time
Identify the limits of the OT role in complex situations
Develop realistic expectations for participant progress
Recognise when MDT collaboration may be required
Part 1
The Role of OT at PACE

OTs support participants to improve:

  • Independence in daily activities
  • Safety within their environment
  • Overall quality of life
  • Participation in meaningful activities
Common areas: Functional capacity assessments · Assistive technology · Home modifications · Capacity building
Part 2
The Reality of Community-Based Practice

Participants may present with:

  • Multiple health conditions
  • Environmental barriers
  • Financial limitations
  • Mental health challenges
  • Social isolation & complex family dynamics
Practice requires adaptation, collaboration, and gradual progress — not immediate solutions.
Part 3
The Purpose of the First Session

The first session typically focuses on:

  • Building rapport
  • Understanding participant goals
  • Gathering information
  • Identifying key priorities
  • Developing shared understanding
Establishing trust is often more valuable than implementing multiple interventions immediately.
Part 4
Progress Not Perfection

Effective therapy often involves:

  • Introducing small strategies
  • Trialling different approaches
  • Reviewing progress over time
  • Adjusting interventions as needed
Focusing on progress rather than perfection helps clinicians manage expectations and reduce unnecessary pressure.
Part 5
Layering Interventions Over Time

Effective therapy may involve:

  • Identifying the most important priorities
  • Introducing strategies gradually
  • Evaluating outcomes
  • Building on previous progress
Part 6
Understanding Scope of Practice

Situations that may extend beyond OT scope:

  • Complex mental health concerns
  • Nutritional issues
  • Communication difficulties
  • Medical management needs
Collaborative care ensures participants receive comprehensive support.
Case-Based Discussion
Case 1
Unrealistic Expectations in Early Sessions

Emma is a new OT who begins discussing multiple strategies with Daniel during session one — energy conservation, equipment options, activity scheduling, and community programs. Daniel appears overwhelmed.

  • Goals: cooking independence, managing fatigue, community participation
  • Emma addressed all areas simultaneously in session one
  • Daniel ended the session unsure where to begin

Discussion Questions

  1. What factors may have contributed to Daniel feeling overwhelmed?
  2. What might a more effective first session have looked like?
  3. How could Emma prioritise Daniel's goals more effectively?
Case 2
Working with Complex Participants

Liam supports a participant who presents with mobility challenges, chronic pain, social isolation, and low motivation. He feels unsure where to start.

Discussion Questions

  1. What factors might influence Liam's clinical decision making?
  2. How could Liam prioritise interventions in this situation?
  3. When might it be helpful to involve the multidisciplinary team?

Key Takeaways

  • Participant-centred care is at the core of the PACE approach
  • Meaningful progress occurs gradually, not immediately
  • Thoughtful prioritisation of interventions is essential
  • Collaboration with the MDT supports comprehensive care
  • Early sessions focus on rapport, not problem-solving
  • Focus on progress, collaboration, and meaningful outcomes
02
Workshop Two
Motivational Interviewing &
Therapeutic Communication
This workshop introduces motivational interviewing and therapeutic communication strategies that help OTs support behaviour change and improve participant engagement.

Learning Objectives

Understand the principles of motivational interviewing
Apply MI communication tools in clinical practice
Identify stages of behaviour change in participants
Recognise common barriers that influence behaviour
Facilitate meaningful conversations toward action
Support long-term engagement in therapy
Part 1
Understanding Motivational Interviewing

MI is a collaborative approach that focuses on:

  • Exploring participant goals
  • Understanding their perspectives
  • Identifying barriers to change
  • Supporting autonomy in decision making
Particularly useful when participants feel unsure, resistant, or overwhelmed by their situation.
Part 2
Behaviour Change in OT

Common reasons for hesitation or resistance:

  • Fear of failure
  • Fatigue or pain
  • Negative healthcare experiences
  • Environmental barriers
  • Low confidence
Approach conversations with empathy and curiosity rather than pressure.
Part 3
Stages of Behaviour Change
Precontemplation
Not yet considering change
Contemplation
Aware of a problem, uncertain about change
Preparation
Beginning to consider possible actions
Action
Actively working toward change
Maintenance
Sustaining new behaviours
Recognising stages helps clinicians tailor conversations to the participant's readiness for change.
Part 4
Communication Tools in Motivational Interviewing

Open Questions

Encourage participants to reflect and share their perspectives.
"What would a good day at home look like for you?"

Affirmations

Acknowledge participant strengths and efforts.
"It sounds like you've already made some positive changes."

Reflective Listening

Demonstrates understanding and clarifies the participant's perspective.
"It sounds like you want to be more independent, but you're worried about making things worse."

Summaries

Reinforce key points and ensure shared understanding.
"So being able to cook independently is really important to you."
Part 5
Addressing Ambivalence

Participants often experience mixed feelings. Rather than persuading, clinicians explore:

"What do you think might improve if things changed?"

Exploring these ideas helps participants articulate their own motivations.

Part 6
Supporting Conceptual Change

Clinicians can support conceptual change by:

  • Acknowledging the participant's perspective
  • Introducing new ideas gradually
  • Linking strategies to meaningful outcomes
  • Reinforcing progress over time
Case-Based Discussion
Case 1
Avoidance of Daily Activities Due to Pain

Maria, 38, avoids daily activities due to fear of worsening her back pain. She wants to cook, spend time with friends, and return to work — but says she'd prefer to "wait until the pain improves."

Discussion Questions

  1. Which stage of behaviour change might Maria be in?
  2. What beliefs or fears may be influencing her behaviour?
  3. What types of questions could help explore her concerns further?
  4. How might MI help Maria move toward considering small changes?
Case 2
Resistance to Home Modifications

David, 62, post-stroke, resists recommended home modifications. He worries the changes will make his home feel "like a hospital" — yet his goals include remaining at home long term and reducing fall risk.

Discussion Questions

  1. What stage of change might David be in regarding home modifications?
  2. What factors might be influencing his resistance?
  3. How might the involvement of his wife influence the conversation?
  4. How could you support David in maintaining a sense of autonomy?

Key Takeaways

  • MI supports participant-led motivation for change
  • Recognising stages of change guides the conversation
  • Open questions, affirmations, and reflections are core tools
  • Ambivalence is normal — explore it, don't argue against it
  • Approach conversations with empathy and curiosity
  • Sustainable change often requires shift in understanding

Workshop Three
Managing Workload &
Preventing Burnout
To support Occupational Therapists in developing sustainable clinical practice habits and maintaining wellbeing while working with complex participants.

Learning Objectives

Recognise common causes of burnout in allied health
Apply strategies for prioritising clinical work
Identify early signs of stress and compassion fatigue
Develop strategies for setting professional boundaries
Understand workload management expectations at PACE
Build habits that support sustainable practice
Part 1
Understanding Burnout in Allied Health

Burnout in allied health occurs due to:

  • High emotional demands
  • Complex client presentations
  • Time pressure
  • Administrative workload
  • Feeling responsible for everything
Early career clinicians are particularly vulnerable — recognising burnout early is essential.
Part 2
Emotional Load in Disability Work

Common clinician responses to emotional load:

  • Feeling personally responsible for participant outcomes
  • Difficulty switching off after work
  • Frustration when progress is slow
  • Feeling overwhelmed by complex cases
These are normal reactions to demanding work — recognise them proactively.
Part 3
Progress Not Perfection

Common early-clinician beliefs to challenge:

  • Feeling they must solve every problem
  • Providing the perfect solution immediately
  • Achieving rapid progress
Effective therapy involves gradual progress, trial and adjustment, and layering strategies over time.
Part 4
Managing Caseload & Priorities

Effective workload management strategies:

  • Identify the most urgent client needs
  • Break complex problems into smaller steps
  • Set realistic timeframes for reports
  • Use supervision to problem-solve difficult cases
Part 5
Setting Professional Boundaries

Boundaries may include:

  • Maintaining appropriate professional roles
  • Recognising limits of scope of practice
  • Setting realistic expectations with participants
  • Avoiding overextension beyond work hours
Strong boundaries support both clinician wellbeing and effective client care.
Part 6 & 7
Seeking Support & Sustainable Habits

Seek support when:

  • Managing complex participants
  • Feeling uncertain about clinical decisions
  • Experiencing emotional stress or overload

Sustainable habits include:

  • Reflecting on challenging cases
  • Maintaining clear work-life boundaries
  • Recognising achievements and progress
Case-Based Discussion
Case 1
Feeling Responsible for Solving Everything

Sarah is researching resources outside work hours, replying to emails at night, and feeling anxious about whether she is doing enough for her participant Michael — a 42-year-old with a spinal cord injury, chronic pain, and low support.

Discussion Questions

  1. What factors may be contributing to Sarah feeling overwhelmed?
  2. What aspects of Michael's situation fall within Sarah's OT role?
  3. Are there opportunities for multidisciplinary support?
  4. What strategies could help Sarah maintain professional boundaries?
Case 2
Difficulty Switching Off After Work

James replays conversations at home, checks emails late at night, and finds it difficult to relax after working with a participant experiencing increasing mental health difficulties.

Discussion Questions

  1. What signs suggest James may be experiencing early burnout?
  2. What strategies could help James manage the emotional impact?
  3. How might supervision or peer discussion help?
  4. What habits could support healthier work-life boundaries?

Key Takeaways

  • Burnout is a risk in disability and community health work
  • Recognise early warning signs and respond proactively
  • Progress over perfection is a sustainable clinical mindset
  • Professional boundaries protect both clinicians and clients
  • Seeking support is normal, expected, and encouraged
  • Sustainable careers require balance across all life domains

Workshop Four
Clinical Reasoning in
OT Practice
This workshop develops clinical reasoning skills when working with participants who present with complex or multifactorial challenges in community and NDIS settings.

Learning Objectives

Understand the role of clinical reasoning in OT practice
Identify key drivers of disability or participation restriction
Apply structured frameworks to guide decision making
Prioritise interventions when multiple challenges are present
Distinguish impairment-focused from functional approaches
Recognise when to trial vs prescribe interventions
Use the PEO model to analyse participation challenges
Part 1
Understanding Clinical Reasoning

Clinical reasoning involves integrating:

  • Clinical knowledge
  • Participant goals
  • Environmental context
  • Professional experience
Part 2
Functional vs Impairment-Based Thinking

Functional thinking asks:

  • What activities does the participant need to do?
  • What is stopping them from doing it?
  • What strategies could improve participation?
Focus on function and participation — not solely on underlying impairments.
Part 3
The PEO Model
P

Person

  • Physical abilities
  • Cognitive capacity
  • Emotional & psychological factors
  • Health conditions
E

Environment

  • Physical environment
  • Social supports
  • Accessibility
  • Cultural and contextual factors
O

Occupation

  • Meaningful activities
  • Daily tasks
  • Roles and routines
Clinical reasoning involves identifying where the mismatch between person, environment, and occupation is occurring.
Part 4
Identifying Key Drivers of Disability

Participants may face challenges from:

  • Physical impairments
  • Environmental barriers
  • Psychological factors
  • Social support limitations
  • Fatigue or pain
Identify the primary driver — don't address everything at once.
Part 5
Prioritising Interventions

Ask:

  • What has the greatest impact on daily life?
  • What is most achievable right now?
  • What aligns with participant goals?
  • What will create early wins?
Address safety risks first, then target high-impact functional tasks.
Part 7
Trialling vs Prescribing Interventions

Trial First When

  • Participant is unsure
  • Multiple options exist
  • Long-term cost is high
  • Functional benefit is unclear

Prescribe When

  • Strong evidence of benefit
  • Clear clinical need
  • Safety concerns are present
Part 6
Managing Complexity in NDIS Participants

Key clinical approach:

  • Simplify where possible
  • Prioritise key goals
  • Introduce changes gradually
  • Collaborate with other professionals
Recognising complexity allows clinicians to set realistic expectations.
Case-Based Discussion
Case 1
Neurological Client

Anna, 55 — stroke 12 months ago. Left-sided weakness, reduced balance, fatigue. Goals: prepare meals, garden, reduce reliance on partner.

Discuss

  1. Key drivers of functional challenges?
  2. How would PEO apply?
  3. What to prioritise first?
Case 2
Mental Health Participant

James, 29 — depression and anxiety. Difficulty maintaining routines, overwhelmed, poor task initiation. Goals: morning routine, home maintenance, community engagement.

Discuss

  1. Factors influencing participation?
  2. How do psychological and environmental factors interact?
  3. Small achievable first steps?
Case 3
Complex Home Environment

Helen, 72 — arthritis, reduced mobility. Narrow hallways, step into shower, limited supports. Recent falls, wants to remain independent.

Discuss

  1. Environmental contributors to risk?
  2. Interventions to prioritise?
  3. When are home mods appropriate?

Key Takeaways

  • Clinical reasoning is a core OT skill
  • Use the PEO model to identify where the mismatch occurs
  • Focus on functional outcomes, not just impairments
  • Identify the primary driver — don't address everything at once
  • Prioritise strategically: safety first, then high-impact tasks
  • Trial interventions when benefit is uncertain

Functional Capacity Assessments

Areas assessed:

  • Activities of daily living
  • Mobility and transfers
  • Cognition
  • Environmental barriers
  • Social and community participation
FCAs must clearly justify why supports are needed and how they improve function.

Part 3
Assistive Technology

Examples: mobility aids, shower equipment, kitchen adaptations, communication devices

Low Cost AT

  • Lower risk, simpler items
  • Minimal assessment
  • Quicker implementation

High Cost AT

  • Higher complexity
  • Detailed assessment
  • Trial and justification
  • Formal funding approval
AT should always be goal-directed, practical, and sustainable.
Part 4
Home Modifications

Examples: grab rails, bathroom modifications, ramps, layout adjustments

Key considerations:

  • Safety
  • Long-term accessibility
  • Participant goals
  • Feasibility of the environment
Part 5
SIL & SDA Overview

Supported Independent Living (SIL)

Support with daily tasks, supervision, building independence in a supported environment.

Specialist Disability Accommodation (SDA)

Specialised housing design for significant physical support needs.

OTs contribute by assessing functional needs, identifying barriers, and providing recommendations.
Part 7
Clinical Documentation – SOAP Notes
S

Subjective

What the participant reports

"Participant reports increased fatigue when cooking."
O

Objective

What you observe

Participant required rest breaks after 5 minutes of standing.
A

Assessment

Your clinical interpretation

Fatigue is limiting task completion and independence.
P

Plan

Next steps

Trial seated meal preparation and introduce energy conservation strategies.
Documentation should clearly link: what is happening → why it matters → what will be done next

Discuss

Key Takeaways

  • Core OT services improve independence, safety, and participation
  • FCAs must justify why supports are needed
  • AT should be goal-directed, practical, and sustainable
  • Home modifications prioritise safety and long-term access
  • Capacity building links directly to real-life activities
  • SOAP notes link observations to clinical decisions

Workshop Six
Working in a
Multidisciplinary Team
This workshop develops clinicians' ability to recognise when multidisciplinary input is required and how to collaborate effectively when supporting participants with complex needs.

Learning Objectives

Understand the role of multidisciplinary care
Apply structured thinking when deciding whether to refer
Identify referral flags indicating need for other disciplines
Communicate effectively within a multidisciplinary team
Understand the roles of key allied health professionals
Maintain clear scope of practice while collaborating
Part 2
Understanding Professional Roles
Exercise Physiologist
Strength, endurance, chronic condition management, physical capacity
Physiotherapist
Movement, pain management, rehabilitation, gait and balance
Psychologist
Mental health, emotional regulation, coping strategies, behaviour change
Positive Behaviour Support
Behaviours of concern, behaviour planning, environmental strategies
Speech Pathologist
Communication, cognitive communication, swallowing
Dietitian
Nutrition, energy levels, diet-related health conditions
The goal is not to do everything yourself, but to ensure the participant receives the right support from the right professional.
Part 3
Identifying Referral Flags

Physical

  • Reduced strength or endurance
  • Mobility limitations
  • Fatigue limiting participation
→ EP or Physio

Mental Health

  • Persistent low mood
  • Anxiety affecting participation
  • Avoidance of daily activities
→ Psychology

Behavioural

  • Behaviours impacting safety
  • Difficulty engaging in therapy
  • High caregiver stress
→ PBS

Communication

  • Difficulty expressing needs
  • Difficulty following instructions
  • Social communication challenges
→ Speech Pathology

Nutrition / Energy

  • Poor dietary intake
  • Low energy levels
  • Significant weight change
→ Dietetics
Part 4
Referral Decision Framework
1
Is this issue within my scope of practice?
2
Is this issue significantly impacting participation?
3
Would another discipline provide better outcomes?
4
Is additional expertise required for safety or effectiveness?
Within scope + manageableContinue OT intervention
Outside scope or complexRefer or collaborate
UncertainDiscuss in supervision
Early referral is often better than delayed referral.
Part 5
Collaborative Case Management

Key strategies:

  • Share relevant information with team members
  • Align on participant goals
  • Coordinate interventions
  • Avoid duplication of services
  • Maintain consistent messaging
Part 7
Maintaining Scope of Practice

While collaborating, clinicians must:

  • Stay within their professional role
  • Avoid responsibilities outside their expertise
  • Recognise limits early
Good clinicians know both what to do — and when to involve others.
Case-Based Discussion
Case 1
Identifying Referral Needs

Tom, 50 — neurological condition. Reduced mobility, fatigue, low physical endurance. Wants to increase activity levels but struggles without fatigue.

Discuss

  1. Factors limiting participation?
  2. Which disciplines to involve?
  3. How could collaboration improve outcomes?
Case 2
Mental Health Barriers

Emily, 35 — anxiety about leaving the house, avoidance of activities, difficulty engaging in therapy. Frequently cancels appointments.

Discuss

  1. Referral flags present?
  2. How is mental health impacting participation?
  3. How could psychology support OT?
Case 3
Coordinating MDT Care

James — spinal cord injury. Currently working with OT, exercise physiologist, and support coordinator toward independence at home and community reintegration.

Discuss

  1. How to coordinate roles?
  2. Communication strategies for collaboration?
  3. How to avoid duplication of services?

Key Takeaways

  • MDT collaboration is essential for complex participants
  • Know the roles of each allied health discipline
  • Recognise referral flags early and act promptly
  • Use a structured framework to guide referral decisions
  • Communicate clearly and consistently across the team
  • The participant experiences coordinated care, not fragmented services
